# Design Doc Review Checklist

| # | Best Practice | Examples | How to Use It | How It Helps | Source(s) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | State both *what* and *why* in the Problem Statement | 1) "Today, the platform does not support X, so customers with need Y cannot do Z"; 2) A one-paragraph problem statement a non-author could repeat back accurately; 3) Naming the specific customer pain before naming any feature | Write a short Problem Statement that names the current gap (what) and the customer or business impact of that gap (why), before describing any solution | Keeps the doc anchored in a real need reviewers can evaluate, instead of starting from "here's what we want to build" | design-review-process.md — "Write initial problem statement; plus goals, non-goals, and constraints" |
| 2 | Describe the customer experience, when the design has one | 1) A short walkthrough of how a customer sets up the feature; 2) How they'd use it day-to-day; 3) How they'd monitor or debug it | Add a Customer Experience section describing setup, use, and monitoring from the customer's point of view | Forces the design to be evaluated against a concrete usage story, not just an internal architecture | design-review-process.md — "It is often useful to include a Customer Experience section here as well" |
| 3 | Phrase goals as testable statements of fact, not proposed solutions | 1) "Allow customers to send traffic using IPv6 global IPs" (goal) vs. "Add a v6-to-v4 proxy" (solution); 2) A goal with a measurable bar like "<30s for a customer to understand the routing behavior"; 3) Goals a reviewer could check a finished feature against | Write each goal as something a shipped solution could be evaluated against, not as the mechanism itself — leave many solutions still possible | Defines the *solution space* before committing to one path, which is the entire point of reviewing goals before designs | design-review-process.md — "1. Goals of this review process" / "Write initial problem statement" |
| 4 | Give every Non-Goal a one-sentence rationale and a one-way/two-way door note | 1) "We will only support 6-to-6 and 4-to-4 — rationale: no customer demand for cross-family NAT; two-way door, can add later"; 2) A non-goal explicitly deferred to a future design; 3) A non-goal pointing to a discussed-but-rejected option in an appendix | For each non-goal, add why it's out of scope in one sentence, and note whether walking it back later would be easy (two-way door) or costly (one-way door) | Prevents wasted discussion time on out-of-scope topics and makes the scope decision itself reviewable, not just assumed | design-review-process.md — "Non-Goals" section under "Write initial problem statement..." |
| 5 | Capture concrete numeric requirements in a Constraints section | 1) "Hard limit of 500 endpoints per resource group"; 2) "Must support 10 trillion concurrent connections"; 3) Latency or capacity numbers stated as ranges when exact figures aren't known yet | Add a Constraints (or Requirements) section listing the numeric bars the solution must clear, separate from the narrative goals | Makes assumptions and scale requirements explicit and testable instead of implicit in someone's head | design-review-process.md — "If you have numeric requirements, I prefer to list those in a separate Constraints (or Requirements) section" |
| 6 | Name the key challenges the design will need to solve | 1) "How do we route packets northbound given the addressing constraints?"; 2) "How do we know the resource is reachable from the internet?"; 3) One challenge per open technical question the design doc will need its own section to resolve | Add a Challenges section listing the hard problems up front, even incompletely, so each gets its own solutions section later | Gives reviewers a map of what the design doc still has to prove, and keeps the doc organized around real open questions | design-review-process.md — "I also sometimes use a Challenges section to describe key problems that the design will have to solve" |
| 7 | Identify Key Allies by role | 1) A filled-in Key Allies table with a named Senior Engineer, PM, SME, and Meeting Host; 2) A Partner Team POC named for each team whose work is needed; 3) A Network Partner named when the project affects the network (e.g. BGP advertisement) | Fill in the Key Allies table (Senior Engineer, PM, SME, Partner Team POC, Syntactic/Writing feedback, Meeting Host, Network Partner if applicable) with actual names, not placeholders | Ensures the author has committed reviewers lined up before asking for feedback, instead of broadcasting to an undefined audience | design-review-process.md — "Identify Key Allies" |
| 8 | Review goals with key allies before any solutioning | 1) A dated note that goals were reviewed with the senior engineer and PM before the design draft started; 2) A summary of that meeting's feedback incorporated into the doc; 3) Evidence the problem statement changed as a result of that review | Hold (or document having held) a goals review with key allies before writing candidate solutions, and fold their feedback into the doc | Catches a wrong problem statement or missing constraint while it's still a one-page fix, not a full design rewrite | design-review-process.md — "Review the problem statement, customer experience, and goals with key allies" |
| 9 | Review goals with the broader/design review team | 1) A note that a 30-minute goals review was held with the wider team; 2) A list of edge cases or concerns raised in that meeting and how they were resolved; 3) New key allies identified as a byproduct of that review | Hold (or document) a wider goals review, capture the edge cases raised, and decide with a senior ally which belong in the doc | Exposes the team to the project early and catches goal-level misses before they require a full rewrite | design-review-process.md — "Review the problem statement, customer experience, and goals with the whole team" |
| 10 | Review goals with partner teams, if any | 1) A note that a goals review was held with a partner team's org; 2) Partner-team feedback reflected in the goals or non-goals; 3) An explicit statement that no partner teams are involved, when true | When another team will do a significant chunk of the work, hold a goals review with that team's org and incorporate their feedback | Surfaces cross-team disagreement or missing context before it becomes a blocker at the design or implementation stage | design-review-process.md — "If a significant chunk of the project will be done by another team, offer to hold a goals review meeting with that team's org" |
| 11 | Present 2+ feasible solutions per key challenge | 1) Two documented options for a hard technical question, each described as viable; 2) A challenge with only one well-defined solution flagged and expanded before review; 3) A speculative option explicitly marked as an untested hypothesis with a named fallback | For each key challenge, write out at least two solutions that could plausibly work, not just the one the author prefers | Lets reviewers weigh trade-offs and catch a fatal flaw in the preferred option while an alternative is already on the table | design-review-process.md — "Red flag: a challenge or problem with only one solution, or only one solution that is well-defined" |
| 12 | Give every candidate solution the same rigor as the eventual pick | 1) Each option written with a full architecture description, not just the "obvious" one; 2) Trade-offs and risks spelled out for all options, not only the underdog; 3) A spike result cited as evidence for feasibility rather than assumed | Write each solution as if it will be the one chosen, with the same level of detail and argumentation | Builds trust that the author actually understands the problem space, rather than pre-deciding and back-filling justification | design-review-process.md — "When you write each solution, assume that it will be the one chosen, and give it the same rigorous level of detail" |
| 13 | Move eliminated or non-viable options to an appendix | 1) A body section that says "see Appendix 1 for a simpler option we rejected"; 2) Options that are no longer relevant kept out of the main flow entirely; 3) A one-line pointer from the body to the appendix, not silent removal | Keep the doc crisp by punting invalid/impossible options to an appendix, with a pointer from the section they came from | Keeps the main design readable and focused on what's actually being proposed, without losing the record of what was considered | design-review-process.md — "Move options that are invalid / impossible to appendixes" |
| 14 | Punt implementation detail to a separate Implementation Design for large-scope work | 1) A high-level doc that defines a strategy sentence ("we will assign each pair a unique port") and links out to a separate implementation doc; 2) A note that low-level docs will follow for each subsystem; 3) A high-level design kept short because details live elsewhere | For projects of significant size (5+ engineers, or both control- and data-plane changes), define the strategy at a high level and move implementation mechanics to dedicated Implementation Design docs | Keeps the high-level design reviewable by a wide audience while still letting implementation detail get the depth it needs elsewhere | design-review-process.md — "Punt implementation details to Implementation Designs" |
| 15 | Keep the high-level design reviewable in about an hour | 1) A design doc under roughly six pages; 2) Solution spaces described without diving into every implementation branch; 3) Detail that doesn't fit routed to an appendix or a linked implementation doc | Aim for a high-level design short enough (roughly ≤6 pages) that a review meeting can cover it in about an hour | A doc nobody can finish reading in one sitting doesn't get a real review — it gets skimmed | design-review-process.md — "it helps keep the high level design short (less than 6 pages), and therefore easily reviewable in an hour-long meeting" |
| 16 | Run the design draft by key allies before wider review | 1) A note that the first draft was reviewed with the same senior engineer/PM/SME allies from the goals phase; 2) Feedback from that review incorporated into a revised draft; 3) A second pass with the same allies before going wider | Run the first design draft by key allies, incorporate their feedback, and repeat until they're satisfied | Catches design-level problems with a small, trusted audience before spending a large meeting's worth of everyone's time | design-review-process.md — "Do the same small-audience, incorporate feedback, re-review again" |
| 17 | Get key allies' buy-in that the doc is ready for a wider audience | 1) An explicit "ready to go wide" confirmation from the senior engineering ally; 2) A revised draft that addressed every open comment from the prior round; 3) No unresolved objections from key allies going into org-wide review | Before scheduling a wider review, confirm with key allies that the doc is ready — don't self-certify | Prevents a design from reaching a large audience only to be sent back for a rewrite that a smaller review would have caught | design-review-process.md — "Run the revised draft by your key allies, get their buy-in, and convince yourselves that the doc is ready for a wider audience" |
| 18 | Review the design with key partner teams, if any | 1) A partner team's sign-off recorded in the doc or its history; 2) Partner-raised concerns reflected in the design; 3) An explicit note that no partner teams are involved, when true | Review the design with each partner team whose work or systems it touches, and incorporate their feedback | Avoids discovering a partner-team conflict only after implementation has started | design-review-process.md — "Review with key partners, if any, and incorporate their feedback" |
| 19 | Hold an org-wide/interested-parties review framed as information-sharing | 1) A final review meeting explicitly scoped as "sharing, not deciding"; 2) Enthusiasm-building framing rather than a go/no-go gate; 3) Feedback from that meeting captured even though the decision was already made upstream | Schedule a final org-wide review whose stated purpose is visibility and enthusiasm, not re-litigating decisions already made with key allies | Keeps a large meeting from turning into an unbounded re-design session, since that work already happened with key allies | design-review-process.md — "Hold a final org-wide / interested-parties review, whose goals are primarily information-sharing and enthusiasm-building, not decision-making" |
| 20 | Estimate the feature before implementation | 1) A Story Sizing (or equivalent) estimate attached to the design; 2) Sizing broken out by milestone; 3) A sizing rationale tied to the design's stated scope | Estimate the feature using the team's sizing method once the design is stable | Turns an approved design into a plannable body of work instead of an open-ended commitment | design-review-process.md — "Estimate the feature using the Story Sizing guide" |
| 21 | Review milestones/work-items with the implementation team | 1) A milestone breakdown reviewed with the engineers who will build it; 2) Work items sized and assigned; 3) Implementation-team feedback that changed the milestone sequencing | Walk the milestones/stories/work-items past the team doing the implementation before work starts | Confirms the plan is buildable by the people actually building it, not just plausible on paper | design-review-process.md — "Review the Milestones/stories/work-items with the team working on the implementation" |
| 22 | Give every review blocker a named owner | 1) A blockers list where each row has a person's name attached; 2) The name being whoever raised the blocker or has the most stake in resolving it; 3) No "TBD" owners left on a blocker after the review | For each blocker raised in review, record a name — the person who raised it or who most needs it resolved | Prevents blockers from silently stalling because nobody was accountable for closing them | design-review-process.md — "A name next to each blocker, representing the person who raised the blocker or has the most vested interest in seeing it resolved" |
| 23 | Follow up on blockers in small, targeted sessions | 1) 1:1s or small (3–5 person) meetings to resolve individual blockers; 2) A dedicated follow-up review only for a blocker significant enough to need one; 3) No re-convening the full org-wide audience just to close a blocker | Resolve blockers through small, focused conversations with the relevant people, escalating to a dedicated follow-up review only when a topic is big enough to warrant one | Keeps blocker resolution fast and avoids spending everyone's time re-discussing something only a few people need to weigh in on | design-review-process.md — "generally 1:1's or tightly focused meetings with the relevant parties (3-5 people) tends to be the most productive" |
| 24 | Leave a written record of *why*, not just *what* | 1) A rationale sentence next to each non-goal; 2) A note explaining why one solution was chosen over another; 3) Decisions traceable to the reasoning in the doc, not only recoverable from someone's memory of a meeting | Write the reasoning behind each significant decision directly into the doc, not just the decision itself | Lets someone six months later understand why the design looks the way it does, without needing to track down the original reviewers | design-review-process.md — "9. Leave a written record of *why* we made the design decisions in the doc" |
